# Contributing to Open Lab

Open Lab is built around a simple idea:

**Practitioners should be able to build, share, challenge, adapt, and improve the tools we use to do our work.**

You do not need to be a developer to contribute.

You do not need to know Git.

You do not need permission to disagree with the original author.

If you've found a better way, that's kind of the point.

## Ways to Contribute

You can:

- improve an existing tool;
- correct something that's wrong;
- challenge an assumption;
- suggest an alternate approach;
- improve instructions or usability;
- contribute examples or variants;
- improve accessibility;
- translate an existing resource;
- report what happened when you used something;
- contribute a tool or framework of your own;
- improve Open Lab itself.

Contributions don't have to start with code or a file.

An observation from actual practice can be just as valuable.

## Open Source First

Open Lab favors open collaboration and inspectable source.

When practical, contributions should include the source material necessary for another practitioner to understand, modify, and improve the work.

The goal is not simply to make finished files available for free.

The goal is to make the **work itself open to improvement**.

## Open Formats First

Use open, non-proprietary formats when practical.

Examples include:

- Markdown (`.md`);
- OpenDocument Text (`.odt`);
- OpenDocument Spreadsheet (`.ods`);
- OpenDocument Presentation (`.odp`);
- CSV;
- JSON;
- YAML;
- SVG;
- plain-text source code;
- other documented open formats appropriate to the artifact.

Proprietary formats such as DOCX, XLSX, and PPTX may be provided when they make the artifact more useful or compatible with the tools practitioners actually use.

They generally should not be the only available source when a practical open alternative exists.

Open Lab cares about the format, not which application you used to create it.

## Before You Share Something

Only contribute material you have the right to share.

Do not submit:

- proprietary employer materials;
- customer or employee information;
- confidential information;
- controlled documents;
- copyrighted commercial products you do not have permission to distribute;
- credentials or secrets;
- lightly disguised versions of proprietary material.

If professional experience taught you something useful:

**Extract the principle. Build something you can legitimately share.**

## Pull Requests

When submitting a change, tell us:

1. What did you change?
2. Why did you change it?
3. What problem does the change solve?
4. Have you used or tested the approach? If so, what did you learn?

You don't need to write an essay.

We just need enough context to understand your thinking.

## Issues

You don't have to know how to modify a tool to contribute something valuable.

If something doesn't work, a question doesn't make sense, an assumption doesn't fit your environment, or you simply have an idea:

**Open an Issue.**

Tell us what you saw.

Someone else may be able to help investigate it, develop the idea, or turn the discussion into a change.

## File Safety

Keep contributed files inspectable whenever practical.

Files should not contain unnecessary:

- macros;
- embedded executables;
- scripts;
- external connections;
- password protection;
- hidden active content.

If active content is genuinely necessary, disclose it clearly and explain why it exists.

## AI-Assisted Contributions

AI-assisted work is welcome.

Use it to brainstorm, challenge an idea, write, code, analyze, prototype, or improve something.

Submitting something means **you** have reviewed it, understand it, and are willing to stand behind the contribution.

"AI generated it" is not validation.

## Licensing

Open Lab uses different open licenses for different kinds of work.

Unless otherwise identified:

- practitioner artifacts and documentation are licensed under **Creative Commons Attribution-ShareAlike 4.0 International (CC BY-SA 4.0)**;
- software components will use an appropriate software license and are intended to use the **MIT License** by default.

By contributing, you agree that your contribution may be distributed under the license applicable to that portion of the repository.

Only contribute material you have the right to license.

Open Lab permits commercial use.

ShareAlike does not mean that you cannot make money using Open Lab resources. It means that when an adaptation covered by CC BY-SA is shared, the applicable attribution and ShareAlike requirements travel with it.

See the repository [LICENSE](LICENSE) for additional information.

## Not Every Difference Needs One Answer

Sometimes two approaches are both useful.

An industry-specific variant, alternate method, or competing model may be more valuable than forcing every idea into one universal tool.

Open Lab is allowed to branch.

## Need Help?

If GitHub, forks, branches, or Pull Requests are unfamiliar, that's fine.

See [How to Contribute](docs/HOW-TO-CONTRIBUTE.md).

You can participate without becoming a Git expert.
